## Program Explanation:

This program is an interactive Python presentation that explains why each of my four Readings 5 sources matters for our crime-scene robot dog project, in which a Unitree Go2 reads bloodstain patterns, walks to the interesting areas, and is trained in simulation before being deployed on the real robot. I built it with Streamlit so it runs in a web browser on Windows. It has Previous/Next buttons, arrow-key navigation, a slide counter, and speaker notes under every slide. The process begins with a title slide and a pipeline diagram (crime-scene simulation → RL training → sim-to-real transfer → Go2 via the SDK), with each stage tagged by the source that supports it. After that, each source gets its own slide with key ideas from my notes and a small live demo. The same code also exports a 14-slide PowerPoint with python-pptx. It runs every demo at its default settings, saves each figure as a PNG, and puts the speaker notes in the notes pane, so I can present from either the app or PowerPoint.

For Hwangbo et al. (2019), I simulated a toy "real" actuator with a series-elastic spring lag, a communication delay, friction, and a torque limit. I logged three minutes of synthetic data at 500 Hz and trained a small MLP to predict torque from a short history (0, 10, 20 and 30 ms ago) of position error and joint velocity, which is a miniature version of the paper's actuator network. The demo compares three models on a held-out 30-second test log: an ideal model that assumes the PD torque is produced instantly, a simple analytical model that adds the torque limit and a guessed lag, and the learned network. A slider changes the hidden delay from 0 to 20 ms so the audience can watch the ideal model's error grow. For the Gurram et al. (2024) survey, I used a 1-D push-cart that has to move 1 m and stop within ±5 cm. A quick random search tunes a PD controller with friction compensation twice: once on one nominal floor (the "fixed" controller) and once across randomized friction, mass, and motor strength (the "randomized" controller). Both are tested on shifted floors (wet, carpet, heavier load, weak motor, and a random mix), and sliders let the audience try to break the fixed controller.

For Kine2Go, the program lists the files in the Hugging Face repository, finds one motion.npy, and downloads only that file instead of the full 31 GB dataset. If the download fails, it automatically loads a bundled sample that I generated myself. That sample is a slow trot built from planned foot paths and two-link inverse kinematics, and it is labelled "SYNTHETIC" on every plot. The slide plots one leg's joint angles, the foot trajectories from columns 36:48, a top-down footfall pattern, and an animated stick figure. For the Unitree SDK, I wrote mock_unitree.py, which copies the shape of the real API (SportClient.Move, LowState with IMU and 12 motor states, and LowCmd with q, dq, kp, kd and tau per motor) without needing CycloneDDS. The demo first switches off sport mode, then runs a 2 ms command loop. Every 20 ms it reads LowState, builds a 45-value observation vector, runs a tiny random-weight network, and writes the q targets into LowCmd, and the app shows both the observation and the outgoing command. A second tab shows the investigation layer: a synthetic stain map creates an "interest" map, and the high-level layer sends Move(vx, vy, vyaw) commands to walk the dog to the most interesting spots.

Each demo is tied back to the project with a "Why it matters for our dog" box. The actuator demo shows that a policy trained on an idealized Go2 could fail on the real dog, so we may need logged Go2 motor data to model its actuators. The domain randomization demo shows why our dog needs randomized training for unknown floors like blood, carpet, and debris. The Kine2Go demo shows that careful search and sniffing-style motions could be imitated from the dog clips, but since it is simulation data it still needs the survey's sim-to-real techniques. The mock SDK demo shows exactly where our investigation policy would plug into the real Go2. Because these are toy models, I labelled every toy model and synthetic dataset on screen. I also kept all settings in config.py and wrote a smoke test that runs every demo and renders every slide.

## Results:

The demos showed the effects I expected. In the actuator demo at the default 10 ms delay, the test-set torque error (RMSE) was 2.23 N·m for the ideal model, 1.70 N·m for the analytical model, and 0.18 N·m for the learned actuator network. When I swept the hidden delay from 0 to 20 ms, the ideal model's error grew from 1.39 to 3.09 N·m and the analytical model's grew from 0.97 to 2.55 N·m. The learned network stayed at about 0.18 to 0.20 N·m because its history input lets it absorb the delay. In the domain randomization demo, both controllers succeeded 100% of the time on the nominal floor, but the fixed controller dropped to 34% on the carpet-like high-friction floor, 69% with a heavier payload, and 58% on the random mix. The randomized controller scored 100% on carpet and on the heavier payload and 91% on the random mix (200 test episodes per condition). Both controllers were 100% on the wet floor and with the weak motor. The randomized controller is not perfect either. In one extra test far outside its training range (no friction, 0.5 kg, 150% motor strength) it failed while the fixed controller succeeded, which shows that the randomization ranges have to cover the conditions the dog will actually see. Every demo ran in a few seconds on a CPU: about 1.7 to 2.5 s for the actuator training, about 1 s for the push-cart, and about 3 s for the Kine2Go and SDK demos including their animations. The full PowerPoint export took about 5 to 6 seconds.

The Kine2Go download did not work in my test environment. The network blocked huggingface.co (the request failed with a proxy error), so the program fell back to the bundled synthetic sample, and the slides and the exported deck show the red "SYNTHETIC SAMPLE – not Kine2Go data" label. That means I could not check the real file's column layout. My notes only give the (T, 61) shape, 60 Hz, and the foot positions in columns 36:48, so the other column meanings in the app are assumptions and are labelled that way. In the mock SDK demo, the policy loop ran 500 command ticks (1 s at 2 ms) with 50 policy updates, and the investigation layer sent 176 non-zero Move commands to visit 2 stain clusters. The main limitations are that the actuator and push-cart demos are toy models, the network in the policy loop is untrained, the SDK is a mock that never talks to a robot, and nothing was run on Go2 hardware. None of the demo numbers are results from the papers. They only illustrate why each source's ideas matter for the crime-scene Go2 project.
